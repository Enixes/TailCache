## Search scope and evidence standard

Exa searches covered direct Java cache comparisons, managed-memory/off-heap research, latency/measurement methodology, venues, publishing policy and graduate-application guidance. Across 22 searches, 137 returned result slots contained 119 distinct URL strings (including duplicates across searches and sources unrelated to the eventual shortlist). This does **not** mean 119 papers were fully reviewed. Selected publisher/author/conference pages, abstracts and available method sections were fetched; follow citation trails before a final novelty claim. Vendor material and practitioner benchmarks are labelled separately from peer-reviewed research.

The broad premise that off-heap storage exchanges GC pressure for access/serialization costs is established. Open versus closed workload behaviour and Java warmup pitfalls are also established. A different pair of cache libraries or adding a rarer percentile does not alone establish research novelty. No exact duplicate of the complete TailCache protocol was identified in this search; that is not proof of absence.

## What could make TailCache a useful contribution?

Candidate contribution, to be tested and refined: **an auditable decision method for on/off-heap caches that accounts for application retention, materialisation allocation, offered load and the chosen tail-latency objective**.

An empirical paper can contribute a finding, counterexample, dataset or reusable method without inventing a cache. It must add transferable knowledge beyond re-demonstrating the established tradeoff. The current ranking reversals are promising observations, not yet a validated general selection rule.

1. **State a falsifiable decision question.** For a particular payload, common retained-data budget and arrival rate, which backend meets a specified latency threshold? Report exceedance probability alongside percentiles and throughput. Keep ordinary get and borrowed/reused buffers separate.
2. **Test the prediction on fresh evidence.** Freeze the 0/104/208 MiB batch before collection; use 104 MiB to test the exploratory bracket rather than fitting a curve and calling it validated. Adaptive later points must be labelled as such. Agreement in three forks is not a significance test.
3. **Quantify mechanism without overstating causality.** Use retained allocation, GC/occupancy, churn and timing-floor evidence; align events only where raw timestamps permit. Separate rare-stall frequency from severity and from scheduled queue amplification. Missing event-level attribution remains a limitation.
4. **Define the resource budget.** Equal JVM heaps and equal application demand are intentional controls, but they do not guarantee equal total process memory: Chronicle uses native storage too. Report available RSS/PSS/native/cgroup evidence and distinguish heap-pressure savings from total-memory savings. An equal-total-memory sensitivity would be new work, not a retrospective reinterpretation of existing runs.
5. **Choose one small generalisation after v1 if warranted.** A selected second host, a different offered rate, a low-pause collector or a small service-level load test may reveal whether the decision transfers. Choose the experiment that addresses the largest review concern; do not expand every factor into a Cartesian matrix.
6. **Make reuse easy.** Release a concise paper, static figures, raw/derived evidence, a quick offline reproduction path, and a clear description of the failed/unqualified original protocol. Maintainer feedback or independent reproduction is evidence of usefulness; citations and adoption cannot be promised in advance.

No need to find a favourable crossover to finish a sound paper. Null, percentile-dependent and workload-specific results can be useful if the question, method and limits are clear. A three-day v1 is a bounded empirical manuscript; it is not a promise of a journal-ready general theory or a high-impact acceptance.

## How journal publication normally works

1. Establish the contribution, compare related work, complete evidence and agree real authorship.
2. Select one venue, check instructions and preprint policy, prepare manuscript/artifacts and cover letter, and submit through its portal.
3. Editorial/technical screening may result in desk rejection or external review.
4. Reviewers critique novelty, soundness, evidence and clarity. The editor may reject, request revisions or accept. Revisions should answer comments point by point and may require targeted new evidence.
5. After acceptance, complete publishing/licensing arrangements, any applicable charges, proofs and production. Peer-reviewed publication is distinct from merely posting a manuscript.

Sources: [Springer Nature's publication workflow](https://www.springernature.com/us/authors/publish-an-article), [editorial stages](https://support.springernature.com/en/support/solutions/articles/6000251301-editorial-process-after-submission), and [ACM author information](https://www.acm.org/publications/authors/information-for-authors). Do not place the same manuscript under peer review at multiple venues at once. A permitted preprint is different from a simultaneous peer-review submission. A later conference-to-journal extension must satisfy the journal's prior-publication/extension rules, not simply resubmit the same paper.

Plan journal review as a longer process, potentially including several revision cycles; no reliable three-day journal acceptance route has been established here. The application deadline should determine when a candid preprint is released, not which outcome the experiment must show.

Ask for a focused assessment of the research contribution and evidence, and an introduction to a systems/performance researcher if appropriate. Existing expertise in another area can help with writing and process; it does not automatically substitute for JVM/performance review.

Provide a one-page brief: question, strongest figure, exact new result versus closest literature, unresolved concerns, repository/manuscript links and the deadline. Ask whether the study is publishable and which single improvement matters most. A former collaborator may be a better starting point than a large cold-email campaign. A professor is not required to be the person who submits the paper. Add a coauthor for substantive intellectual work and manuscript responsibility, not access to a journal or an affiliation. [ACM's authorship criteria](https://www.acm.org/publications/authors/information-for-authors) require substantial intellectual contribution and drafting/revision; advice short of authorship can be acknowledged appropriately. All listed authors must review and approve the work.

**Application value is a judgment, not a guaranteed admissions effect.** A well-explained systems study can document question formulation, independent implementation, measurement judgment, response to failed hypotheses, honest uncertainty and technical writing. For research-oriented programmes, it can support a coherent systems-research narrative alongside the earlier ML paper. Recommender testimony about the user's actual decisions and contribution makes that evidence easier to assess. Taught master's programmes may weigh research differently; check each programme rather than assuming a paper is required.

For example, [Stanford's recommendation guidance](https://gradadmissions.stanford.edu/apply/recommendations) asks referees about intellectual independence, analytical thinking and advanced-study potential, and recommends a professor familiar with the applicant's academic work. This supports the value of informed testimony; it is not evidence that TailCache will change admission odds by a particular amount.

Keep status precise: manuscript in preparation, publicly posted preprint, submitted manuscript, under external review, accepted paper and published paper are different states. Do not describe a Zenodo DOI as peer-review endorsement, or a submission as acceptance. Strong ownership and a credible explanation may be more useful than adding a weak publication solely for its count; that is our strategic judgment, not an admissions rule.

Strengths to preserve: fresh JVM repetitions, value/trace integrity checks, source-matched comparisons, independently decoded histograms, raw GC/allocation evidence, full-tail reporting, negative findings and transparent warning/exclusion history. Remaining limitations are contribution novelty and transferability, not simply benchmark count: the focused headline cohort is one host/heap/collector/arrival rate/primary seed with three pairs per point, short extreme-tail windows and explicit source groups. The original campaign's 64 robustness invocations are real evidence for its own regimes; they do not independently confirm the later scheduled-response crossover. Original unconditional-protocol failure must remain explicit.

The existing harness is a substantial implemented artifact, but it currently locates finite observed brackets through controlled sweeps. It is not yet a validated general automatic crossover detector or a predictor with demonstrated accuracy across unseen systems. That would be a possible new research contribution requiring explicit evaluation of accuracy, uncertainty, cost and failure cases against baselines.

Near-term recommendation remains a scoped preprint plus the closest empirical venue, rather than expecting a TSE/TOCS decision before applications. For any journal extension, first write the new knowledge in one sentence, get a systems/performance researcher to challenge it, and collect only the evidence needed to test it. Prestige language such as “instantly establishes an elite researcher” is not a defensible claim about admissions or individual technical ability.
